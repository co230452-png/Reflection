Second Week • August 8, 2026

Security Threats and Social Engineering Techniques and Psychological Tactic

This week, I learned about the 12 categories of threats to information security and how threats are not limited to hacking or malware. They can also come from human errors, technical failures, natural disasters, theft, and other factors. It reframed how I think about security risk — not as a single adversary trying to break in, but as a much wider range of things that can go wrong, many of which have nothing to do with an attacker at all.

I also gained a new understanding of phishing and social engineering techniques. Before this lesson, I thought that emails, text messages, and phone calls that pretend to be someone from an organization were all simply called phishing. I learned that they have different names depending on how they are carried out and who they target. Vishing is done through phone calls, smishing through SMS, spear phishing targets a specific individual or organization, and whaling targets high-ranking individuals.

I also learned about the psychological tactics used in social engineering. Some of these were already familiar to me or I had even encountered some of them before, such as greed, authority, fear, scarcity, and urgency. However, during the lessons I learned more about how these tactics are intentionally used to influence people's decisions.

I would like to read more about the different social engineering techniques, especially how attackers combine different techniques and psychological tactics to make their attacks more convincing. I am also interested in learning more about how organizations protect their employees from these types of threats.

I did not experience any major confusion or challenges with the topics discussed this week. The concepts were understandable, especially because some of the social engineering tactics were things I had already encountered or was familiar with.

Risk Assessment

During our lessons and activities about Risk Assessment, I learned how important it is to identify and evaluate the information assets of an organization. Before assessing possible risks, we first need to know what assets the organization has and how important each one is to its operations. These assets may include information, computers, systems, and other resources that the organization uses. Understanding these assets is important because they are the things we need to protect.

I also learned that we need to give ratings to these assets based on different categories, such as their confidentiality, integrity, and availability, as well as their importance to the daily operations of the organization. This helps us understand the value of each asset and determine which ones need more protection and attention. Not all assets have the same importance, so properly rating them helps us set priorities and decide which assets could have a greater impact on the organization if something happens to them.

Another thing I learned was how to identify the possible risks that could affect each asset and list the current controls or safeguards that are already in place. These controls help protect the assets from possible risks. We then examine each risk together with its existing controls to identify possible vulnerabilities. For example, if an organization faces a certain risk but has no safeguards to prevent or reduce it, this can be considered a vulnerability. The same applies when the existing controls are not enough to properly address the risk or when they are not being applied properly.

Through this activity, I learned that identifying vulnerabilities requires us to look closely at the possible risks and the safeguards currently available. It is not enough to simply list the risks because we also need to understand what the organization is already doing to manage them and where its weaknesses are. This helps us determine which areas need additional controls or improvements to reduce the chances of those risks happening or lessen their possible effects.

Overall, this activity helped me understand that risk assessment involves identifying assets, evaluating their importance, listing possible risks, checking existing safeguards, and finding weaknesses that need attention. I learned that this process helps an organization understand its current security situation and decide what actions are needed to better protect its important assets. It also taught me the importance of properly assessing risks because knowing the possible problems and weaknesses can help an organization prepare before they cause serious damage to its operations.
